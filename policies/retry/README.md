# retry

Policies for deciding how long to wait before trying again. The delay curve
is the part everyone already knows. The part that decides outcomes is where the
randomness goes.

**Start with [exponential full jitter](exponential-full-jitter/)** unless a row
below applies to you. It cuts the peak simultaneous retries roughly sixfold
against un-jittered backoff, for one extra line of code.

## Choosing one

| If you need | Use | Because |
|---|---|---|
| A sensible default | **[full jitter](exponential-full-jitter/)** | Same curve as exponential, spread across a fleet: 2.6% peak against 15.1%. |
| The backoff to actually back off | **[equal jitter](equal-jitter/)** | Guarantees a floor of half the ceiling. Full jitter can return zero every time. |
| To disperse a fleet as thoroughly as possible | **[decorrelated jitter](decorrelated-jitter/)** | Whole schedules diverge, not just individual attempts: **1.1%** peak, the lowest here. |
| A delay sequence reproducible without randomness | **[exponential](exponential/)** | `nextDelay(3)` is 400 every time. It also synchronises every client. |
| To honour a server that told you when to return | **[Retry-After-aware](retry-after-aware/)** | 99.6% success against 19.9%, and the worst herd of any policy here. |
| The naive baseline, to measure against | **[constant](constant/)** | `sleep(100)` in a loop. Included to be measured, not recommended. |

## The two numbers, and why they trade

Every row in the table below reports two numbers. A **success rate** is what
the client gets. A **peak retry share**, the largest fraction of all retries
landing inside one 10 ms window, is what the recovering service pays.

They pull against each other, and the uncomfortable case is the recommended
policy: **full jitter succeeds *less* often than plain exponential** (0.199
against 0.400) because a uniform draw from `[0, ceiling]` averages half the
ceiling, so it exhausts the same attempt budget in half the elapsed time. It is
still the right default, because the axis it wins on, not arriving as one wave,
is what turns an outage into an outage that does not end.

A reader who found that number independently would rightly distrust a page that
had hidden it, so `backoff-policies.test.ts` asserts the ordering and this page
states it.

## Why every success rate looks low

The reference configuration is `baseMs` 100, `capMs` 10,000, `maxAttempts` 8:
what a default HTTP client does. That budget covers about **12.7 seconds** of
un-jittered exponential backoff, against outages of up to thirty. Most episodes
therefore end in failure for every policy, and constant backoff, patient for
seven hundred milliseconds in total, succeeds under 2% of the time.

Those numbers are accurate rather than broken. Eight attempts at a 100 ms base
does not survive a 30-second outage, and a benchmark that hid that by choosing a
kinder outage would be flattering every policy equally while distinguishing
none.

## Traces

One workload: `outage-30s`, 1,000 independent outages of up to 30 seconds behind
a service that still fails one attempt in ten once recovered.

**Every episode's first attempt is at t=0**, so the thousand episodes are also a
thousand clients that failed simultaneously, which is precisely the situation
jitter exists for, and what makes the peak retry share meaningful. Full
specification in
[`TRACES.md`](../../packages/core/src/domains/retry/TRACES.md).

### Reading the table

**Success rate ranks the patient, not the good.** A policy that waits longer
reaches more recoveries within eight attempts. Exponential beats full jitter
here for exactly that reason, and a policy that retried forever would score 1.0
while being the reason the service stayed down.

**Peak retry share ranks the considerate.** Constant and exponential are
statistically indistinguishable on it, at 14.4% and 15.1%, despite completely
different delay curves, which is the finding worth taking away: **backing off
exponentially converts a continuous herd into a periodic one. It does not
disperse it.** Dispersal needs randomness.

**Decorrelated jitter wins the herd and loses the clock.** At 1.1% it is the
most considerate policy here, and it has the longest mean time to success, for
the same reason: its walk climbs by about 1.5x a step against exponential's 2x,
so within one attempt budget it covers less elapsed time. Two clients that
started together are at very different delays a few attempts in.

**Retry-After-aware is the outlier in both directions.** It succeeds 99.6% of
the time because it is *told* how long the outage lasts, and it records a 23.4%
peak, worse than doing nothing at all. 835 of the 1,000 episodes have an outage
longer than the five-second cap on what a server will ask for, so all 835 get
the identical hint and return together. The clamp creates the herd, and real
services clamp the same way. Its own page explains why the benchmark
*understates* that risk.

## The interface

```ts
nextDelay(attempt: number, error: RetryError): number | null
```

`attempt` is 1-based and is the number of the attempt that just failed. Null is
a decision to give up, not an error.

The `Rng` is supplied at construction rather than threaded through `nextDelay`,
which departs from Every other domain here supplies randomness
at construction, including the C vtable's `create`, and making retry the
exception would put a `pb_rng *` on the C hot path for no benefit. The property
that matters, that a policy never reaches for a global source, is unchanged.

[Decorrelated jitter](decorrelated-jitter/) is the only policy here that holds
state between calls, and its object's lifetime is therefore the retry sequence:
one constructed inside a retry loop restarts its walk every attempt and
degenerates into a fixed-range draw.

See [`interface.ts`](../../packages/core/src/domains/retry/interface.ts) for the
full contract.
